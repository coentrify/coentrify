# Motion Onset

A movement that starts draws the eye. Steady motion does not draw it by itself, and a change slow enough can go unnoticed altogether.

## Why

A world can move all the time without pulling at the player, and every start calls for attention, wanted or not.

## The Evidence

- Abrams and Christ 2003, Motion onset captures attention, Psychological Science 14(5), 427-432, [doi:10.1111/1467-9280.01458](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.01458). A letter that had just started to move was found first. A letter that kept moving had no advantage over a still one.
- Smith and Abrams 2018, Motion onset really does capture attention, Attention, Perception, and Psychophysics 80(7), 1775-1784, [doi:10.3758/s13414-018-1548-1](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13414-018-1548-1). A reply to a challenge, upholding the capture.
- Simons, Franconeri and Reimer 2000, Change blindness in the absence of a visual disruption, Perception 29(10), 1143-1154, [doi:10.1068/p3104](https://doi.org/10.1068/p3104). A gradual change drew no attention, and large changes went unnoticed.
- How solid: solid in the lab, with letters and shapes on a screen. Untested in a game world.

## Limits

- The research is about starts. Whether a stop draws the eye by itself is less settled, so weighing a stop like a start errs on the careful side.
- Motion that keeps running can distract a player who struggles to hold attention, and some motion makes a player with a balance disorder dizzy. The web's accessibility rules ask for a way to pause motion that runs beside other content, and to turn off motion an interaction sets off (WCAG 2.2, criteria 2.2.2 and 2.3.3).
