# The Peak-End Rule

People remember a short experience largely by its most intense moment and its end, and its length counts for little. Knowing something is the last raises its enjoyment.

## Why

The end of a session, a level or a game weighs heavily in how the player remembers it.

## The Evidence

- Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier 1993, When more pain is preferred to less: adding a better end, Psychological Science 4(6), 401-405, [doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x).
- Alaybek et al. 2022, All's well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect, Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes 170, 104149, [doi:10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.obhdp.2022.104149). Strong, and about as strong as the average of all moments.
- Do, Rupert and Wolford 2008, Evaluations of pleasurable experiences: the peak-end rule, Psychonomic Bulletin and Review 15(1), 96-98, [doi:10.3758/PBR.15.1.96](https://doi.org/10.3758/PBR.15.1.96). A high followed by a lesser high was rated lower than the high alone.
- O'Brien and Ellsworth 2012, Saving the last for best, Psychological Science 23(2), 163-165, [doi:10.1177/0956797611427408](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611427408).
- How solid: solid for short episodes.

## Limits

- It holds for a session, a level or a set piece. For a whole game played over weeks it weakens (Miron-Shatz 2009, [doi:10.1037/a0015295](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0015295)).
- Ending on a high is supported. Leaving the player wanting more is untested.
