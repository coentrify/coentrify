# Spatial Memory

People learn where things are on a screen as they use them, and go there from memory instead of searching. A layout that keeps its places keeps that knowledge useful, and one that moves things sends people back to searching.

## Why

A player who knows where a control is reaches it without looking, often with the hand already on its way.

## The Evidence

- Ehret 2002, Learning where to look: location learning in graphical user interfaces, CHI, 211-218, [doi:10.1145/503376.503414](https://doi.org/10.1145/503376.503414). People learn the places of controls as they use them.
- Findlater and McGrenere 2004, A comparison of static, adaptive, and adaptable menus, CHI, 89-96, [doi:10.1145/985692.985704](https://doi.org/10.1145/985692.985704). A menu whose items stayed put was faster than one that rearranged them by use.
- Scarr, Cockburn, Gutwin and Malacria 2013, Testing the robustness and performance of spatially consistent interfaces, CHI, 3139-3148, [doi:10.1145/2470654.2466430](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2466430). A layout that kept its places beat one that reflowed.
- How solid: consistent across small lab studies of menus and command layouts. Untested in games.

## Limits

- The studies cover finding a control again, over minutes and sessions. A control that moves while the player clicks is the plainer case: the click lands where the control was.
