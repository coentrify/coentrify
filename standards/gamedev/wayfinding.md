# Wayfinding

Relevant for: a world the player finds the way in.

A player finds the way by a mental map built from five elements, after Kevin Lynch's *The Image of the City* (1960): paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks. A world is easy to find the way in, and to remember, when the five are strong and clear.

Rests on: [Cognitive Maps](../psychology/cognitive-maps.md).

## Why

A player who can read the world finds the way without a minimap or an arrow, so the world guides without yellow paint ([No Yellow Paint](game-design-laws-guidance.md#no-yellow-paint)).

## The Five Elements

- Paths - the channels the player moves along. A good path has a clear identity and a clear direction.
- Edges - linear boundaries the player does not move along: a coast, a wall, a river. Some can be crossed and some cannot.
- Districts - larger areas with a shared character that the player feels inside of.
- Nodes - strategic points the player enters: a junction, a square, a station.
- Landmarks - points of reference the player sees and does not enter. A distant one orients, a local one marks a spot.

## How To Apply

- Nodes are the glue, and the element a sketch forgets. Every region connects to its neighbors through at least one clear node, and a node is memorable in itself.
- In a first sketch of the world, tag every part as one of the five. A missing kind points at a gap.
- Every district has a clear edge and at least one node. Every major path has a clear destination.
- Every district gets one distant landmark, seen from the main road, and a few local ones.
- In a greybox playtest, ask which landmarks the player found the way by. Where there are none, add a distant landmark.
- The sketch-map test: after a playtest, the player draws the map from memory. What is forgotten is weak, and what is drawn first works ([Playtest Files](../records/playtest-files.md)).
