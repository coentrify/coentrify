# Game Design Laws: Story

Laws about what the game says, hints and teaches. The index and the way a game uses them are in [Game Design Laws](game-design-laws.md).

## Show, Don't Tell

Never tell the player what happened, what to feel or what to experience; let the player experience it. A bad guy is discovered by doing a bad thing to the player, so the frustration is felt rather than announced. A good thing arrives as emotionless stats that actually do something, not as lore or explanation; the player attaches their own judgment. When words are needed, they are short and easy to understand, preferably voiced, subtitled and animated, without being too spot on.

## No Yellow Paint

Yellow paint is steering applied too much, the exemplary case being the painted ledge: an arrow, a hint, a helper or a quest log that does the player's thinking. Steering that is needed is not yellow paint; where it seems needed, the design gets another look first. One thing on screen with a clear hover and interactable state needs no word to click it. Rich context clues drive the narration without deciding or thinking for the player. Two questions test it at any moment: what am I looking at, and what am I expected to do here.

- A hint is a context clue: it encloses what is already on screen in a narrative and gives it meaning, ambiguous and indirect, innocent without the context. It never motivates.
- Lines that cheer the player on, almost through, keep going, nearly there, do the opposite of a clue: they say nothing about the world and treat the player as someone who needs encouragement, which nobody does.
- Telling the player what to do belongs to a concept the player has not met before, and then once. A mechanic that has been introduced is never hinted again, and the line goes as soon as the concept has landed.

## Answer The Player's Next Question

What the game shows is support for the player's decisions: it answers the question the player would ask at that moment, guiding their thoughts without steering them ([Walk The User's Journey](../ui/walk-the-users-journey.md)). So every word and number is weighed by what it means to the player there, never by what the system holds.

- A value means something only against a reference: 0 means nothing, Free says this is a purchase, things here cost something, and this one costs nothing.
- A purchase says what it gets the player, so they know where to look next.
- A tiered value shows where the player stands, where it goes and the difference, and makes plain whether a step adds to the last or multiplies it, and what it comes to in the game.
- Nothing is told in terms the player has not met yet: a currency is introduced as something to get, the wallet at 0, before a price is told in it; no buff on something that does not exist yet.
- It serves both ways of thinking: the explorer asking what can be done here, and the follower asking what the game wants done.

## Worth Reading

Applies to a game with dialog. Part of what makes a game good is that the player can miss something, which gives them a reason to read.

- no dialog has arrows to walk its lines again
- a line missed stays missed

## Dialog Gives Context

Applies to a game with dialog. Dialog accompanies what is on screen as its narrative: it gives the player the context the picture alone leaves out, what is going on, who is there and what is at stake.

- The context comes from characters speaking about what they see and want: "Who is that?" "I don't know, he looks scary. Let's get out of here before he sees us."
- A guide checks in, explains and asks whether the player is ready.
- Dialog gives context, never instructions: what to do stays with [No Yellow Paint](#no-yellow-paint).
- A narrator explaining what happened or what to feel stays out ([Show, Don't Tell](#show-dont-tell)).

## Make Yourself

Applies to a game with a player character. The player can personalize the game to reflect themselves: the character can look like them, their hair, their face, their build, and the world carries what they stand for. Being represented matters more than a look of a certain vibe or graphical realism; a player who cannot make themselves is missing something.

The player needs the ability and the space to represent themselves: the personalization options, and no steering of their judgment. The game stays as neutral as it can, so the character can relate. That is no indifference when a bad guy is being bad; it means not clouding it with external reasoning or noise, the writer's judgment among them.

## Adopt, Repeat, Confirm (ARC)

A pattern to apply, not a strict rule. Every new thing is introduced in five steps, one new thing per link.

- Awareness: the feature is seen, no action yet
- Trigger: the conditions for the action are met
- Adopt: the player does it once
- Repeat: the player does it again
- Confirm: the third time confirms the action as a skill; it is a test that can be failed, an invitation, and the game just moves on

Confirm overlaps the next link's Awareness, so the pattern widens the horizon as it chains.
