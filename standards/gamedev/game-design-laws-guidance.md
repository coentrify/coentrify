# Game Design Laws: Guidance

Laws about what the game hints and teaches. The index and the way a game uses them are in [Game Design Laws](game-design-laws.md).

## No Yellow Paint

Yellow paint is steering applied too much, the exemplary case being the painted ledge: an arrow, a hint, a helper or a quest log that does the player's thinking. Steering that is needed is not yellow paint. Where it seems needed, the design gets another look first. One thing on screen with a clear hover and interactable state needs no word to click it. Rich context clues drive the narration without deciding or thinking for the player. Two questions test it at any moment: what am I looking at, and what am I expected to do here.

- A hint is a context clue: it encloses what is already on screen in a narrative and gives it meaning, ambiguous and indirect, innocent without the context. It never motivates.
- A line never judges or pushes the player: no well done, keep going or nearly there. Those say nothing about the world, and praise for an easy success can read as a low opinion of the player. Feedback states what happened, such as a triple kill, and a character voices its own feeling, such as a companion's anger at an enemy: get him! A line that works on the player's feelings, warmly or harshly, is manipulation.
- Telling the player what to do belongs to a concept the player has not met before, and then once. A mechanic that has been introduced is never hinted again, and the line goes as soon as the concept has landed.

Rests on: [Curiosity As An Information Gap](../psychology/curiosity-as-an-information-gap.md), [Rewards And Feedback](../psychology/rewards-and-feedback.md).

## Answer The Player's Next Question

What the game shows is support for the player's decisions: it answers the question the player would ask at that moment, guiding their thoughts without steering them ([Walk The User's Journey](../ui/walk-the-users-journey.md)). So every word and number is weighed by what it means to the player there, never by what the system holds.

- A value means something only against a reference: 0 means nothing, Free says this is a purchase, things here cost something, and this one costs nothing.
- A purchase says what it gets the player, so they know where to look next.
- A tiered value shows where the player stands, where it goes and the difference, and makes plain whether a step adds to the last or multiplies it, and what it comes to in the game.
- Nothing is told in terms the player has not met yet: a currency is introduced as something to get, the wallet at 0, before a price is told in it. No buff on something that does not exist yet.
- It serves both ways of thinking: the explorer asking what can be done here, and the follower asking what the game wants done.

Rests on: [Cognitive Load](../psychology/cognitive-load.md), [Reference Dependence](../psychology/reference-dependence.md).

## Adopt, Repeat, Confirm (ARC)

Every new thing is introduced in five steps, shaped to fit the thing, one new thing per link. The steps fit naturally into the world or the game, with time between the uses, so they never hold the player's hand or slow anyone down. An experienced player walks right through them and gets their bearings again. Overloading a new player with everything at once is far worse.

- Awareness: the feature is seen, no action yet
- Trigger: the conditions for the action are met
- Adopt: the player does it once
- Repeat: the player does it again, some time later
- Confirm: the third time, later again and without help, confirms the action as a skill. It is an invitation, and a test that can be failed, or nothing is learned. The game just moves on

A failed Confirm teaches through the result of the action alone. Nothing tells the player what they should have done, judges them, or cheers them on with a you'll get it next time ([No Yellow Paint](#no-yellow-paint)).

Confirm overlaps the next link's Awareness, so the pattern widens the horizon as it chains.

Rests on: [Cognitive Load](../psychology/cognitive-load.md), [Retrieval Practice](../psychology/retrieval-practice.md), [Rewards And Feedback](../psychology/rewards-and-feedback.md).

## Tracks, Never Instructs

A quest log is a to-do list, never a job description: it tracks, and never instructs. A task comes with a reason the player cares about: helping the witch cook a magic potion, the player hands her the apple she asks for. A bare help the witch, when the player neither knows nor cares, is weak design, and so is a game rigged so the player wants to follow its instructions.

Rests on: [Reactance](../psychology/reactance.md), [Autonomy, Competence And Relatedness](../psychology/autonomy-competence-and-relatedness.md).
