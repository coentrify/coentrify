# Game Design Laws: World

Laws about the world the player moves through: its secrets, its reactions and its friction. The index and the way a game uses them are in [Game Design Laws](game-design-laws.md).

## Fair Surprise

The world has rules the player does not know yet, and the player finds them by playing, never by being told.

- A surprise answers something the player did. It never comes out of nothing.
- It warns once, in the world's own way: a sign of strength for the player who looks.
- It may hurt, at a cost the game has already made normal.
- It opens something: a path of discovery, a questline, a reward for the player who learns to use it.
- After the first time the risk is readable. The player sees it coming and can partly steer it.
- A risk that can never be read is a dice roll, and teaches nothing.

An example of the surprise: an ordinary-looking creature, attacked by accident, turns out far stronger than anything met so far. It takes the first hit and gives a warning no other character gives. The player who goes on loses the fight, wakes up where every lost fight ends, and has found the start of a hidden questline.

An example of the readable risk: speeding with forbidden goods can get the player pulled over. The player chose the speed, and can still pay, talk or run. A fine that lands at random leaves nothing to learn.

[Full Control](game-design-laws-experience.md#full-control) holds, since the surprise answers the player's own action. [Know What Is Coming](game-design-laws-progress.md#know-what-is-coming) keeps the progress visible, and the world keeps its secrets.

Rests on: [Controllability](../psychology/controllability.md), [Predictability](../psychology/predictability.md), [Curiosity As An Information Gap](../psychology/curiosity-as-an-information-gap.md).

## The World Reacts

The world acknowledges the player's presence: an action leaves a trace that can be seen. A trace may fade again. The reaction is what counts.

## Friction In The World

Friction is something the player does in the world, never a menu or a wait.

Rests on: [Flow](../psychology/flow.md).

## No Artificial Gates

Relevant for: a world that unlocks.

An unlock is never gated arbitrarily. What is out of reach is out of reach for a reason the world gives: the player has no connections there yet, or it lies too far out. No invisible wall, and no lock the world does not explain.

## Contrast

Relevant for: an open world, and a world or a player that grows.

A world lives by the contrast between dense and sparse, and between danger and safety, in space and through time. In space, a city feels full and busy only beside an empty forest, and the forest feels empty only beside the city. Through time, an empty or small start sets off a full or large end: the world fills, the player grows, and the growth is felt only against where it began ([Earned Progress](game-design-laws-progress.md#earned-progress)). A world crammed full everywhere, or full from the start, loses both.

- A sparse area holds few points of interest, maybe one: an artifact hidden deep in the forest. It feels quiet, yet the player senses something could be out there, and the wish to explore is fed at irregular moments ([Exploration Is Rewarded](game-design-laws-experience.md#exploration-is-rewarded)).
- The contrast pulls the player toward the center and away from looking for the edges: the closer to the center, the busier and more exciting, as naturally as possible.
- A drive out of a busy area and its pressure, such as the police on the player's tail, feels like an escape, from the surroundings alone.
- Danger and safety set each other off, as a deadline and a breather from the clock do ([Natural Breaks](game-design-laws-experience.md#natural-breaks)). An area that keeps demanding focus, such as being chased or drawing enemies, lies apart from a safe haven, with a no-man's land in between. In the haven the player sinks into an activity, such as a minigame, without worrying about an attack: immersion nested inside the game. What the player needs comes from the busy area, with its risk, its variance and its rewards for exploring, and the player returns to the haven, in a loop.
- Never miles and miles of nothing: an explorer gets back to the busy world reasonably quickly.
- A game names its poles, in space and through time, and where the player meets each.
